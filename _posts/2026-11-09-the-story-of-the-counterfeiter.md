Long ago, in the countryside, lived a painter. He was a humble man who made his living as a shepherd.

He would tend the flock by day, and at night, every night, he would paint. The painter burned candle after expensive candle to illuminate the canvas just enough, for long enough, to create.

His paintings mesmerized the village. Each day, he would place a new painting in his workshop, which was what he called his home. Each day, an adoring crowd would stroll by his workshop to see the new works. Aristocrats pined for them, but he never sold. When the painter could tell a piece struck someone, he would offer the painting to them on the spot — no cost.

Soon, looking wasn’t enough for some. Thieves began slipping into his workshop in the early morning to steal pieces. At first, one or two. Later, the workshop would be picked clean frequently. The painter’s stolen works were sold in neighboring villages. Amateur reproductions followed quickly.

One of the painter’s works stood above the rest: *The Kállos*. It depicted Medusa dancing with her sisters at sunrise. Retellings held it was the most beautiful painting anyone in the region had ever seen. It reportedly drew tears from a visiting royal; he was heard softly weeping in his carriage afterwards.

*The Kállos* was stolen within weeks of being hung on his workshop’s wall. This time, the theft wasn’t hidden in darkness. An art dealer came from the royal city to see *The Kállos* and pressured the painter into handing the painting over to him. Most locals said it was grabbed, not handed. Within the year, fakes started showing up, first in far-flung city markets and then countries away.

The painter couldn’t visit the market without seeing a fake of one of his works. He kept burning his shepherd’s wage on candles week after week.

Every so often, the painter would arrive to find his workshop ransacked. Some paintings would be gone, most left in shreds on the floor. Village rumor let slip that those who destroyed the painter’s work did it in glee.

One day, a woman was admiring his latest painting at the workshop and noticed him by the blank place on the wall where *The Kállos* used to hang. The painter studied the spot as if he could still see *The Kállos* before him. She nudged him: “Doesn’t it bother you, seeing other people destroying your paintings? Copying them? Stealing them?”

“No,” he beamed, “because I can make the original.”
